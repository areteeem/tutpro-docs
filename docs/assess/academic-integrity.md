# Academic integrity and reducing answer sharing

Online delivery does not make cheating impossible, and no browser setting can prove a learner’s intent. Build integrity through clear expectations, thoughtful questions, reasonable controls, and a fair review process. Use the least intrusive measures that fit the stakes.

## Start with the assessment design

1. Ask students to apply a concept to a new example, explain a choice, show intermediate steps, or connect ideas to their own class material. These prompts are harder to answer by copying a single key than recall-only questions.
2. Use several equivalent prompts or versions when the class is large. A question bank with random selection is a pattern supported by platforms such as Moodle; use it only when questions assess the same outcomes and have comparable difficulty. [Moodle quiz settings](https://docs.moodle.org/403/en/Quiz_settings) · [Building a Moodle quiz](https://docs.moodle.org/403/en/Building_Quiz)
3. Make open-book expectations explicit. If tools, notes, collaboration, or generative AI are allowed, say what is allowed and what students must disclose. If they are not allowed, give a clear reason and a way to ask about accessibility or exceptional circumstances.
4. Use more than one kind of evidence for consequential learning: drafts, worked examples, classroom practice, short reflections, or a brief follow-up discussion can help explain a result without relying on invasive monitoring.

## Add proportionate delivery controls

TutPro Assess exposes controls such as response availability and close time, attempt limits, question and answer shuffling, question timing, backtracking, progress display, answer reveal timing, full-screen testing options, and selection/copy restrictions where enabled. Choose the combination that supports the task:

- A close time and attempt limit can clarify when work is accepted; allow enough time and communicate time-zone assumptions.
- Shuffling makes copied answer sequences less reusable. It does not stop a student from sharing the content of a question or using another device.
- Random order is most useful when questions do not depend on earlier questions. Keep linked passages, multi-part prompts, and accessibility needs in mind.
- Disable copying or selection only for a specific reason. It can hinder assistive technology and legitimate study behavior, and cannot prevent photos or external devices.
- Full-screen or warning controls are limited signals. Explain them before the test and offer an alternative when a student’s device, disability, or environment makes them impractical.
- Avoid surprise monitoring. If proctoring is considered for a high-stakes exam, weigh privacy, accessibility, bias, false positives, and data retention, and keep a human appeal process. A review of online proctoring research describes these as recurring concerns. [Review of automated online exam proctoring](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44217-026-01224-3)

## Review results fairly

Compare unusual results with the learning evidence available to you, not with a single automated signal. Similarity scores identify matching text, not plagiarism; a high score can reflect quotations and a low score does not rule out contract cheating. [Turnitin: similarity and plagiarism](https://guides.turnitin.com/hc/en-us/articles/34400565079053-Turnitin-and-plagiarism)

If a response raises a concern, speak with the student, review their reasoning or working, check whether the prompt or permitted resources were clear, and apply the same policy consistently. Preserve the student’s privacy and document the basis for any decision. Do not treat fast answers, a changed window, a similarity percentage, or a detector result as proof by itself.

Research results are context-dependent. A randomized online-exam study observed behavior consistent with peer information-sharing between exam rounds; a generic ethics reminder did not change the measured outcome in that study. This supports designing equivalent but varied forms and limiting answer-key exposure, but it does not prove that shuffling alone reduces cheating in every class. [Barcelona School of Economics field experiment](https://bse.eu/research/publications/academic-integrity-line-exams-evidence-randomized-field-experiment)

## Use generative AI thoughtfully

AI can produce plausible answers to some standard test questions. A study of medical multiple-choice questions analyzed question vulnerability to ChatGPT-based answering; its findings are specific to the question set and evaluation period. Use it as a reason to keep prompts tied to course context and update questions, not as a universal score of AI accuracy. [Study of test-question vulnerability](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14881)

Give students a concrete AI policy: state which tools are permitted, what assistance must be acknowledged, what data must not be pasted into public tools, and how students can demonstrate their own understanding. UNESCO’s guidance emphasizes human-centered use, privacy, and age-appropriate safeguards in education. [UNESCO guidance for generative AI in education and research](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=67098)

## A practical setup in TutPro Assess

1. Draft questions that test application and reasoning, then preview the student view.
2. Set availability, attempt count, time limits, shuffle options, and result visibility to match the assessment purpose.
3. Explain allowed materials, AI use, timing, and how to request help before sharing the link or assignment.
4. Keep answer reveal and result publication intentional. Review ambiguous automatic scores before releasing results.
5. If the task is high stakes, consider a brief oral explanation or a second piece of evidence instead of escalating to surveillance by default.
